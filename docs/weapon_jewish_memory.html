The Weapon They Call Jewish Memory

June 6, 2026

On the evening of June 5, 2026, at a campaign rally in Bar Harbor, Maine, Graham Platner — the Democratic candidate for U.S. Senate, his campaign reeling from allegations about his treatment of former girlfriends, facing a primary in four days — told hundreds of cheering supporters: "As every single piece of that past and journey gets dug up, litigated, and weaponized, you have my back."

The New York Times reported it under the headline: "Amid Mounting Democratic Concern, Platner Says His Past Is Being 'Weaponized.'" I read it the next morning and felt something harden in me — not surprise, but recognition. I knew that word. I knew exactly where it came from and what it is designed to do: recast the ordinary operation of accountability as sinister aggression. A justice system prosecuting crime through ordinary process becomes "weaponized." Moral judgment applied to immoral conduct becomes "weaponized." Memory refusing to forget what a symbol meant and who wore it becomes "weaponized." The accusation flips the burden from the conduct to the critic.

Donald Trump didn't invent the concept of a defendant reframing accountability as persecution, but he industrialized it, repeating "weaponized" so many times and in so many contexts that his press secretary, Karoline Leavitt, was eventually reduced to arguing at a White House briefing, "It is not weaponizing the Department of Justice to demand accountability for those who weaponized the Department of Justice." The word had, by then, eaten itself. Now a progressive Democrat, running explicitly against everything Trump represents, has dug into that same rhetorical toolkit to protect himself from scrutiny of the Nazi symbol he wore on his chest for eighteen years.

The essential facts are not in dispute. Until he began his campaign for the United States Senate, Graham Platner wore on his chest a tattoo of the Totenkopf — the death's head emblem of Hitler's Schutzstaffel, the SS. The SS was not merely a military or paramilitary organization; it was the ideological police force of the Nazi state, the apparatus of racial terror, and the organization that ran the concentration camps. The Totenkopf appeared on the caps of the men who stood at the gates of Auschwitz, unloaded the human cargo, and administered the machinery of extermination.

The SS death's head is not a symbol that requires esoteric knowledge to identify. It is the symbol worn by the worst cinematic Nazi villains of the last forty years, not just in solemn films like Schindler's List but also in Indiana Jones and the Last Crusade and Inglourious Basterds. One does not need a Ph.D. in European history to recognize it; one needs only to have consumed the same American pop culture as every other man of Platner's generation. In fairness, perhaps Platner missed all of that. Maybe he thought the SS officers who wore this stylized skull-and-crossbones were pirates.

Platner says he chose the design from a tattoo parlor's "flash wall" during a drunken night in 2007 with fellow Marines in Croatia, and that for years he remained entirely unaware of its origin and meaning. He claims he was "appalled" when he learned what the image was. He did not, however, do anything about it for eighteen years, not until a video surfaced during his Senate campaign of him dancing shirtless at a wedding, the Totenkopf visible on his chest, and reporters began calling. Within days of the story breaking, he had it inked over with a Celtic knot and a dog, chosen, he explained, because it better reflects his connection to "nature and animals" than "the violence he partook in as a young man."

Platner could have said, "I inked over my tattoo because it was a Nazi symbol and I was horrified to have carried it." Instead he explains it away as a youthful attraction to violence, as if the problem with the symbol were its martial character rather than its specific identity. But underneath Platner's Celtic canine, the SS logo remains exactly as he requested it.

The question of what Platner knew about his Totenkopf, and when he knew it, deserves scrutiny like any other self-serving claim of ignorance. He points out that he wore the insignia through military service, background checks, and a security-clearance review, and notes, apparently as exculpation, that none of these processes flagged it. But the failure of a screener to identify a tattoo, or to do anything about it, tells us nothing about whether the person bearing it knew what it meant. A man who spent years in the Marine Corps, deployed to Iraq and Afghanistan, now asks us to believe that in eighteen years no one — not a single fellow Marine, not a single veteran, not a single barroom companion — ever looked at the death's head on his chest and told him what it was. I leave the plausibility of that to the reader.

My name is Shuman. I have never known of a Shuman in America who is not a Jew. My claim to Jewish identity is, by some religious definitions, subject to interpretation — I am patrilineal; my mother is a Scottish Presbyterian. But my father resolved this question for me long ago, in one of the moments when he was not eliding our heritage entirely. "Hitler would have put you in a boxcar to the camps," he told me one boozy evening, "as he did with our cousins we never knew." The Nuremberg Laws did not ask for your grandmother's papers. They looked at your name. The men who wore the Totenkopf would not have debated my claim to Jewishness.

When I say that I cannot forgive a candidate who wore that symbol, plainly and unaltered, on his body for eighteen years, I am not being emotional. I am being precise. I am identifying a symbol worn by the murderers of my unknown cousins and declining to treat its presence on a Senate candidate's chest as a matter requiring nuance. I have been told that this position constitutes "handwringing" — that the sophisticated response is to weigh the emblem Platner indelibly inked into his skin against the importance of defeating the Republican incumbent and arrive, with appropriately mature political judgment, at the conclusion that the greater good requires setting it aside.

Consider what would have happened if the tattoo had been a more familiar Nazi image. Had Platner chosen a swastika, or a portrait of Hitler, there would be no conversation; his candidacy would have ended within forty-eight hours. No Croatian "flash wall" story, no "appalled" statement, no Celtic-knot-plus-dog coverup would have survived contact with those images. Bernie Sanders could not have said, as he did about the Totenkopf, that "we've got to focus on a system which in many ways is collapsing" rather than "a tattoo." Elizabeth Warren could not have called Platner "the real deal." Chuck Schumer could not have answered reporters' questions with, "We're going to beat Susan Collins and take back the Senate.... Any other subject you've got?"

Platner's defenders are not arguing that Nazi symbolism is forgivable; they are arguing that this particular Nazi symbol sits below the threshold of the unforgivable ones. They have constructed a hierarchy, and the criterion for placement within it is not historical significance, not the body count associated with the symbol, not the specificity of the genocidal intent — it is a cynical evaluation of whether Maine Democratic primary voters will recognize it, or care. The Totenkopf was worn on the caps of the men who ran the death camps, and the possibility that voters may not know that or may dismiss it is being treated by Platner's defenders as a political asset.

What makes this especially painful is who the defenders are. Chuck Schumer is a Jew. Bernie Sanders is a Jew. Neither is ignorant of what the Totenkopf is or who wore it. They know. They have made the calculation that a Senate seat is worth swallowing this outrage. Only a few years ago, this calculation would have been unthinkable — the moral cost too obvious, the political cost too high. That it is now not only worth considering, but, in fact, politically ideal, tells us something about how far the line has moved and how quickly.

There is a bargain that operates in progressive political spaces, and it is worth stating plainly because it is rarely stated at all. The bargain says: your Jewish history is acknowledged, antisemitism is condemned, and you are welcome here — but your identity may not make operational demands. It may exist as background, as a fact about you that everyone is glad to affirm in the abstract, but it must not inconvenience the coalition. It may not draw a line when the coalition decides that line costs too much, and if you draw it anyway, you are the problem.

In a recent discussion with a friend of a friend, I held my line, and I was told I was engaging in "handwringing." This word, whether precisely chosen or uttered carelessly, does not engage with the substance of my objection. It reframes the act of objection as neurotic excess, a failure of the sophistication that would allow me to see past my feelings and support the greater good. The obstacle, it says, is not the behavior that prompted the grievance; it is the person who raises the grievance.

Compare how political passion is received when it comes from a constituency treated as electorally indispensable. Loud, performative tribalism, when practiced by the right people with the right flag on their trucks and the right slogans at the rally, is coded as authenticity, values, conviction, patriotism. Nobody calls their objections handwringing or pearl-clutching. No one suggests that these voters should modulate their attachments for the sake of coalition discipline. For some, identity is permitted to make demands on politics; for others, it is admonished as counterproductive.

I am a Jew. The symbol Platner wore was worn by the men who murdered my cousins — cousins whose names I do not know, whose lives are lost to me except through my father's assurance that Hitler would have put me on the same train. My objection to a candidate's Totenkopf tattoo is not a political position, or a calculation to be weighed against Senate arithmetic, nor is it an inappropriate emotional reaction I must rein in on behalf of someone else's electoral strategy.

This is not handwringing. This is the line I am holding.
